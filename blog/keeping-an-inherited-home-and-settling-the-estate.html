How One Heir Can Keep a Florida Home and Pay the Others

Florida families often ask how one heir keeps an inherited home while fairly paying the others. Here's how the process may work for your family.

By Jim Blackburn, NMLS #1072866 · October 13, 2026

One heir can keep an inherited Florida home and pay the others by using a refinance — often called an estate buyout refinance — to borrow against the home's equity and distribute each co-heir's share in cash. The heir keeping the home takes title, qualifies for a new mortgage in their own name, and uses the loan proceeds to settle what is owed to siblings or other beneficiaries. This process may work for many Florida families, though each situation depends on equity, creditworthiness, and estate status.

Losing a parent or loved one is hard enough without the added pressure of figuring out what happens to the family home. Maybe you grew up there. Maybe a sibling lives nearby and wants to hold on to it too. Whatever the mix of wishes in your family, you are not alone — and there is a path forward that may let one person keep the home while treating everyone fairly.

How does an estate buyout refinance work in Florida?

An estate buyout refinance works by replacing any existing mortgage — or creating a new one on a free-and-clear property — so the heir keeping the home can pull out cash to pay the others their share. Florida families often use this approach because it avoids a forced sale while still giving every heir what they are entitled to under the estate.

Here is a general picture of how the process tends to unfold:

  1. Estate settles or reaches a point where title can transfer. Probate may need to be complete, or at least substantially progressed, before a lender will fund a refinance.
  2. The home is appraised. The appraised value determines how much equity exists and how much may be available through a cash-out refinance.
  3. The heir applying for the loan qualifies on their own financial profile. Income, credit, and debt obligations all factor in.
  4. Loan closes and proceeds are distributed. The heir keeping the home signs for the new mortgage; co-heirs receive their buyout funds at or after closing.
  5. Title is updated to reflect sole ownership. Once the loan is funded, the heir keeping the home is the owner of record.

Each step involves coordination between an estate attorney, a title company, and a licensed mortgage originator. Getting those parties working together early tends to make the whole process smoother.

What determines how much each heir receives?

The share each heir receives is typically determined by the will, the trust, or Florida intestacy law if no estate plan exists. The total equity in the home — appraised value minus any outstanding liens — is the pool being divided. If the home appraises at a certain amount and carries no existing mortgage, the full equity may be available to split among heirs.

Keep in mind that not all equity is necessarily accessible through a refinance. Lenders set limits on how much of a home's value can be borrowed against, so the math matters. Exploring your options for keeping or settling the estate is a good starting point.

How does Florida law affect the inherited home process?

Florida's probate process governs how inherited property transfers to heirs, and the timeline can range from a few months to considerably longer depending on estate complexity. Florida does not impose a state estate tax, which may simplify some financial planning aspects, though federal rules still apply — always consult a tax professional.

Florida also has a homestead designation that can affect how a primary residence is inherited and what can be done with it. If the deceased lived in the home as their primary residence and it qualifies as homestead property, there are specific rules about who can inherit it. An estate attorney licensed in Florida is the right person to walk your family through these details.

What if siblings disagree about keeping the home?

Disagreement among heirs is common, and it does not have to end in a courtroom. Many Florida families work through mediation or simply take more time to reach a consensus once the financial picture becomes clear. Knowing that a buyout is possible — and understanding roughly what each heir could receive — often helps move conversations forward.

If no agreement is reached, Florida courts may order a partition sale, which means the property is sold and proceeds divided. That outcome is rarely ideal for anyone who wanted to keep the home. Exploring options like a buyout early, before positions become entrenched, may help your family avoid that path. You can also look at our overview of decisions facing inherited homeowners to understand the full range of paths available.

Could the heir keeping the home also benefit from current housing trends?

Florida's housing market has remained a topic of significant attention, with ongoing conversations about affordability, financing access, and what homeownership means in different parts of the state. For an heir considering keeping a family property, the existing equity built up over years of ownership could be a meaningful resource — potentially more accessible than starting from scratch in today's environment.

That context does not change the fundamentals of an estate buyout, but it is worth knowing that the home you inherited may carry real financial value that works in your family's favor as you negotiate a fair settlement.

What should Florida heirs do first?

Before making any commitments, gather the basics: a copy of the will or trust, a recent mortgage statement if the home has a loan, and a sense of each heir's wishes. Then connect with an estate attorney to confirm where things stand legally, and speak with a licensed mortgage originator to understand what a refinance might look like for your situation.

Frequently asked questions

Do all heirs have to agree before one can keep the inherited home?
Generally, yes — all co-heirs need to reach an agreement before one heir can take sole ownership. Without agreement, a court process called a partition action could force a sale. Working with an estate attorney and a knowledgeable mortgage team early may help families avoid that outcome.
What happens to an existing mortgage on an inherited Florida home?
Federal law generally allows an heir to assume or refinance an inherited home's existing mortgage without triggering a due-on-sale clause, though lenders still have requirements. Speaking with a licensed loan originator like Jim Blackburn, NMLS #1072866, could help clarify your options based on the loan type and current servicer.
How long does the estate settlement process take in Florida?
Florida probate timelines vary widely depending on estate complexity, whether a will exists, and whether heirs are in agreement. Some informal administrations close in a few months, while contested estates could take considerably longer. An estate attorney familiar with Florida law is the right resource for timeline guidance.
Can an heir refinance an inherited home before probate closes?
In most cases, a refinance requires clear title, which typically means probate needs to be at least substantially complete. Some lenders may work with heirs during probate in limited circumstances, but this varies. Reaching out to a team like Stairway Mortgage early helps you understand where you stand.
What if the home has no mortgage — can an heir still borrow against it to pay siblings?
Yes, an heir who takes ownership of an unencumbered property could potentially use a cash-out refinance to access equity and pay co-heirs their share. The heir would need to qualify for the new loan based on their own financial profile. This is a common path for Florida families settling inherited estates.
Is a buyout taxable for the heir receiving the payment in Florida?
Tax treatment of estate buyouts depends on individual circumstances, and this is a question for a CPA or tax attorney, not a mortgage team. Florida does not have a state estate tax, which may simplify some aspects of the process, but federal rules still apply. Always seek qualified tax advice before making decisions.
What documents do Florida heirs typically need to start a buyout refinance?
Lenders generally ask for the death certificate, letters of administration or testamentary, a copy of the will or trust, and standard income and asset documentation. Title work confirming the transfer of ownership is also required. Starting to gather these early can help the process move more smoothly.
